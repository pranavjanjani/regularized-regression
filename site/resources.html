Reading

Primary sources — read these

Hoerl & Kennard (1970), Ridge Regression: Biased Estimation for Nonorthogonal Problems

Technometrics 12(1). The original, framing ridge as a response to ill-conditioning rather than as a Bayesian object. Worth reading because the Bayesian interpretation came later.

Tibshirani (1996), Regression Shrinkage and Selection via the Lasso

JRSS-B 58(1). Introduces the $\ell_1$ penalty and, in §5, the Bayesian Laplace-prior reading we use throughout. Short and unusually readable.

Zou & Hastie (2005), Regularization and Variable Selection via the Elastic Net

JRSS-B 67(2). The grouping-effect theorem from Theory §6.1, the double-shrinkage correction, and the gene expression example. Task 4 reproduces this paper in miniature.

Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani (2010), Regularization Paths for Generalized Linear Models via Coordinate Descent

J. Statistical Software 33(1). The algorithm implemented in Task 3, by the people who made it the default. Read §2 before writing your solver.

McMahan et al. (2013), Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches

KDD 2013. A candid account of running a penalized linear model at production scale, including the parts about memory rather than accuracy. See Applications §1.

Textbooks

  • Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 3 is the canonical treatment. Figures 3.8 and 3.11 are the coefficient paths you reproduce; Figure 3.11 is the diamond-and-ellipse picture from Theory §5.2. Freely available from the authors.
  • Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning — §3.1–3.5 for the Bayesian treatment. §3.3 has the sequential-posterior figure that Walkthrough 6 animates.
  • Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — Chapter 11. The clearest modern statement of the MAP-equals-penalty correspondence.
  • Boyd & Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization — §3 and §4 if you want the subdifferential argument in Theory §5.1 done properly.

Formula sheet

Everything you need for the assignment, on one screen.

Likelihood and MAP

$$\hat w_{\text{MLE}} = \argmax_w \log p(y\mid X,w), \qquad \hat w_{\text{MAP}} = \argmax_w \big[\log p(y\mid X,w) + \log p(w)\big]$$ $$\text{penalty} = -2\sigma^2 \log p(w)$$

The four estimators

$$\hat w_{\text{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$$ $$\hat w_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y, \qquad \lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$$ $$\hat w_{\text{lasso}} : \quad w_j \leftarrow \frac{S(\rho_j,\ \lambda/2)}{\lVert x_j\rVert^2}, \qquad S(\rho,\gamma) = \operatorname{sign}(\rho)\max(|\rho|-\gamma,\,0)$$ $$\hat w_{\text{EN}} : \quad w_j \leftarrow \frac{S(\rho_j,\ \lambda_1)}{\lVert x_j\rVert^2 + \lambda_2}$$

Partial residual and $\rho_j$

$$r^{(j)} = y - \sum_{k \neq j} x_k w_k, \qquad \rho_j = x_j^\top r^{(j)}$$

With standardized columns and the $1/n$ convention, $\rho_j = b_j - \sum_{k\neq j} A_{jk} w_k$ where $A = X^\top X/n$ and $b = X^\top y/n$.

Gaussian posterior

$$\Sigma = \Big(\tfrac{X^\top X}{\sigma^2} + \tfrac{I}{\tau^2}\Big)^{-1}, \qquad \mu = \tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\Sigma X^\top y, \qquad \hat w_j \pm 1.96\sqrt{\Sigma_{jj}}$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(y_\star) = x_\star^\top \Sigma x_\star + \sigma^2$$

Diagnostics

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat w_{\text{OLS}}) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}, \qquad \text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1-R_j^2}, \qquad \mathrm{df}(\lambda) = \sum_j \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}$$

For two standardized features, $\det(X^\top X/n) = 1-r^2$ and $\text{VIF} = 1/(1-r^2)$ for both.

Glossary

Terms this course uses in a specific sense. Where students routinely conflate two ideas, both are listed together.

Shrinkage vs. selection

Shrinkage pulls coefficients toward zero without reaching it, so the model still uses every feature. Selection sets coefficients to exactly zero, removing features. Ridge only shrinks; Lasso does both. The difference is not one of degree: a coefficient of $10^{-9}$ still has to be computed, stored and explained.

Bias, variance, and why regularization trades one for the other

OLS is unbiased: averaged over many datasets it hits the truth. It can still be useless, because its variance — how much it moves between datasets — can be large when $X^\top X$ is ill-conditioned. Penalizing introduces bias in exchange for a reduction in variance. Expected squared error is bias² + variance + irreducible noise, and only the first two are yours to trade.

Collinearity, condition number, VIF

Three views of one problem. Collinearity: some column is nearly a linear combination of others. Condition number $d_{\max}/d_{\min}$ of $X$: above ~30 you should be uneasy. VIF: how much feature $j$'s coefficient variance is inflated by the others; above 5–10 is the usual alarm. All three are measuring the smallest singular value from different angles.

Cross-validation vs. a held-out test set

Cross-validation is for choosing: hyperparameters, feature sets, model class. The test set is for reporting, once. The moment you use it to pick anything it stops being a test set and its error becomes optimistic. Here: CV on trips 1–150, report on 151–200.

Censoring

An observation is censored when you know the value is at least (or at most) something, but not what it actually is. The 21 trips at the ₹150 cap are censored: the willingness-to-price was higher and was never recorded. Ordinary least squares treats ₹150 as the truth, which biases coefficients on the drivers of extreme surge toward zero. Tobit regression is the standard fix; noticing the problem is what this course asks of you.

Standardization, and why penalties demand it

$\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ treats all coefficients as comparable, but a coefficient's size depends on its feature's units. Measure traffic in m/s rather than km/h and its coefficient changes by 3.6×, its penalty by 13×, and the fitted model with them, though nothing about the world changed. Ordinary least squares is scale-equivariant; every penalized method is not.

The one-standard-error rule

CV curves are estimates with their own noise. Rather than the exact minimum, take the simplest model (largest λ) whose CV error is within one standard error of it. You give up a negligible amount of estimated accuracy for a simpler model, and stop resolving differences the CV cannot detect.

Pitfalls, in the order students hit them

  1. Forgetting to standardize before a penalized fit, so one feature is penalized thousands of times more than another purely because of its units.
  2. Standardizing before splitting, leaking the test set's mean and variance into training. Fit the scaler on the training rows; apply it to the test rows.
  3. Selecting λ on the test set, then reporting the test error as if it were honest.
  4. Mismatched λ conventions between your solver and scikit-learn — the $1/n$ and the factor of 2 in $\lambda/2$ are the usual culprits. Find the factor; do not tune until it matches.
  5. Reading a Lasso selection as causal. Among correlated features the choice is close to arbitrary and changes under resampling. Bootstrap it first.
  6. Quoting α to four significant figures off a CV curve with a flat bottom.
  7. Comparing Ridge and Lasso at the same α and concluding one shrinks harder. They parameterise differently. Compare at equal ‖w‖₁ or equal degrees of freedom, as the home page slider does.
  8. Reporting that a model "explains" something because a coefficient is large, without checking whether its credible interval covers zero.

A note on the case study

The surge-pricing system described on this site is a teaching reconstruction built on synthetic data, not a description of any company's production system. The structure — spatial grids, short-horizon demand and supply forecasts, itemised additive pricing, price caps — reflects publicly described industry practice. The features, coefficients and dataset are ours. See the note at the top of the case study and the evidence tags on the applications page.
