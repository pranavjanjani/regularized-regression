1 · The label, and what making it cost us

There is no binary column in the dataset, so we build one: high_surge = surge_additive_inr > ₹100. That is an honest operational target — above roughly ₹100 the dispatch team wants an alert — but it is also a deliberate loss of information, and the lab measures that cost in §6 rather than hiding it.

Always quote the base rate

A model that predicts "no alert" for every cell scores — accuracy. Any accuracy you report has to beat that number before it means anything, and at extreme thresholds it becomes almost impossible to beat. This is why §5 uses ROC and log-loss instead.

2 · Why a straight line will not do

Fit ordinary least squares to a 0/1 label and it will happily predict −0.3 and 1.4. Logistic regression puts the linear score through a sigmoid, so the output is always a probability:

$$ P(y=1 \mid x) = \sigma(x^\top w + b) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(x^\top w + b)}} $$

5 · The model gives a probability; the threshold is your decision

A fitted classifier does not output a class. It outputs a probability, and someone has to choose where to cut. That choice is a business trade-off between missed alerts and false alarms, and it belongs in the policy layer — exactly like the price cap in the case study.

What to take from this lab

The penalties behave exactly as they did for linear regression, because nothing about the prior changed — only the likelihood. That is the whole point of having derived MAP once, generally: swap in a Bernoulli likelihood and you get penalized logistic regression for free, with the same λ = σ²/τ² reasoning about where the penalty weight comes from.

The second lesson is about the label. If thresholding the regression matches the purpose-built classifier, then collapsing a rich continuous outcome into two buckets bought you nothing and cost you resolution. Check the last row of the table before reaching for a classifier next time.
