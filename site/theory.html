Lecture notes · Modules 1–7

Every penalty is a prior

The four estimators, derived in one continuous argument and checked against the Mumbai surge data in the lab.

1 · The linear model and least squares

We observe $n$ trips. Trip $i$ has features $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ (peak-hour flag, rain flag, traffic speed, drivers available) and an outcome $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$, the surge charged in rupees. We posit

$$ y_i = x_i^\top w + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n. $$

Stack the rows into the design matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ and the outcomes into $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, so that $y = Xw + \varepsilon$. Least squares chooses $w$ to minimise the residual sum of squares

$$ \mathrm{RSS}(w) = \lVert y - Xw \rVert_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - x_i^\top w\right)^2 .$$
Expand: $\mathrm{RSS}(w) = y^\top y - 2w^\top X^\top y + w^\top X^\top X w$.
Differentiate: $\nabla_w \mathrm{RSS} = -2X^\top y + 2X^\top X w$.
Set to zero — the normal equations: $X^\top X\,w = X^\top y$.
If $X^\top X$ is invertible, $\;\hat w_{\text{OLS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$.

The Hessian $2X^\top X \succeq 0$, so this is a global minimum. Geometrically $X\hat w = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto the column space of $X$, and the residual is perpendicular to every column.

1.1 · Where it breaks: collinearity

Everything above hinges on inverting $X^\top X$. Write the singular value decomposition $X = U D V^\top$ with $D = \mathrm{diag}(d_1 \ge \dots \ge d_p)$. Then

$$ \hat w_{\text{OLS}} = V D^{-1} U^\top y, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat w_{\text{OLS}}) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1} = \sigma^2 V D^{-2} V^\top .$$

A small singular value $d_j$ enters as $d_j^{-2}$. In the Mumbai data is_peak, is_rain and traffic_speed move together: rain is likelier in the evening peak, and both slow traffic. Their pairwise correlations run from $-0.75$ to $0.66$ (see the correlation matrix). $X^\top X$ is then nearly singular, and least squares can shift weight between the rain and peak coefficients with almost no change in fit.

The failure you will reproduce in Task 1

Fit OLS on is_rain and traffic_speed using only the first six trips. Those two columns are correlated at $-0.94$ there, and you get $\hat w = (10.8,\, -34.2)$. Add one more trip and it becomes $(21.7,\, -23.8)$ — a swing of ₹11 per standard deviation from a single observation. This is not a bug in your code; it is $D^{-2}$ doing exactly what the algebra above says it will.

Worse is available. Take is_rain and is_bad_weather over the first ten trips: in that stretch every rainy trip also had traffic below 25 km/h, so the two columns are identical, $\det(X^\top X) = 0$, and the maximum likelihood estimate does not exist at all. Not "is unstable" — does not exist. Ridge, as we are about to see, returns a perfectly sensible answer on the same data.

2 · Maximum likelihood

So far least squares was an aesthetic choice. Make it a statistical one: assume the noise is independent Gaussian, $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. Then

$$ p(y \mid X, w, \sigma^2) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - x_i^\top w)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right). $$

Take logs — the likelihood is a product, and products are miserable to differentiate:

$$ \ell(w,\sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) \;-\; \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^\top w)^2 . $$

Only the last term involves $w$, and it is $-\mathrm{RSS}(w)/2\sigma^2$. Maximising over $w$ is therefore identical to minimising RSS:

$$ \hat w_{\text{MLE}} = \argmin_w \; \lVert y - Xw\rVert_2^2 = \hat w_{\text{OLS}}. $$
The loss is a modelling assumption

Least squares is not an alternative to maximum likelihood; under Gaussian noise it is maximum likelihood. Change the noise model and the loss changes with it. Laplace noise gives least absolute deviations; Bernoulli outcomes give cross-entropy.

Differentiating $\ell$ with respect to $\sigma^2$ and setting to zero gives $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} = \mathrm{RSS}(\hat w)/n$, which is biased downward; the unbiased estimator divides by $n - p$ instead. In the assignment we simply fix $\sigma = 10$ ₹, because the data generating process is known.

MLE is consistent, asymptotically efficient and invariant to reparameterisation. Its weakness matters here: when $p$ approaches $n$, or columns are collinear, maximising fit to the data in hand is the wrong instruction.

3 · Bayes, and the MAP estimator

Treat $w$ as a random variable with a prior $p(w)$ encoding what you believed before seeing data. Bayes' theorem gives the posterior

$$ p(w \mid y, X) = \frac{p(y \mid X, w)\,p(w)}{p(y\mid X)} \;\propto\; p(y\mid X,w)\,p(w). $$

The maximum a posteriori estimate is the posterior mode. Taking logs turns the product into a sum, and the normalising constant drops out because it does not depend on $w$:

$$ \hat w_{\text{MAP}} = \argmax_w \Big[\log p(y\mid X,w) + \log p(w)\Big] = \argmin_w \Big[\underbrace{\lVert y - Xw\rVert_2^2}_{\text{data fit}} \;-\; \underbrace{2\sigma^2 \log p(w)}_{\text{penalty}}\Big]. $$
The key identity

$-\log(\text{prior})$ is the penalty. Choose a prior, take its negative log, and you have a regularizer. Every method below follows from this equation; only the density on $w$ changes.

Walkthrough 2 runs this construction step by step: it starts from the likelihood, multiplies in a prior, negates, and watches $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$ appear. It also lets you dial $\tau$ and see the estimate travel from the MLE to zero.

4 · Ridge regression — the Gaussian prior

Put an isotropic Gaussian prior on the coefficients, $w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2)$ independently. Then

$$ \log p(w) = -\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_{j=1}^p w_j^2 + \text{const}, $$

and substituting into the MAP objective:

$$ \hat w_{\text{ridge}} = \argmin_w \; \lVert y - Xw\rVert_2^2 + \lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2 , \qquad \boxed{\lambda = \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}} $$

The penalty weight is the ratio of noise variance to prior variance, not a free parameter. A confident prior (small $\tau^2$) or noisy data (large $\sigma^2$) both increase shrinkage. In the assignment $\sigma = 10$ and $\tau = 1$, so $\lambda = 100$.

Objective: $J(w) = (y-Xw)^\top(y-Xw) + \lambda w^\top w$.
Gradient: $\nabla J = -2X^\top y + 2X^\top X w + 2\lambda w$.
Set to zero: $(X^\top X + \lambda I)\,w = X^\top y$.
$\;\hat w_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y$.

Since $X^\top X \succeq 0$, adding $\lambda I$ with $\lambda > 0$ raises every eigenvalue to at least $\lambda$, so the inverse always exists, even when $p > n$ and OLS is undefined.

4.1 · What shrinkage does, in the SVD basis

Using $X = UDV^\top$, the fitted values become

$$ X\hat w_{\text{ridge}} = \sum_{j=1}^{p} u_j \,\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\, u_j^\top y .$$

Each principal direction is multiplied by $d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda) \in (0,1)$. Directions with large $d_j$, where the data genuinely varies, are barely touched; directions with tiny $d_j$, the ill-conditioned combinations, are crushed. Ridge shrinks the unreliable directions hardest.

4.2 · Why correlated features share weight

Suppose two columns are identical, $x_1 = x_2$, and the best total effect is $c$. Any split $(a, c-a)$ fits equally well, so OLS is indifferent — the solution is not unique. Ridge breaks the tie by minimising $a^2 + (c-a)^2$, which is smallest at $a = c/2$. Equal split.

This is what Task 2 shows: at $\lambda = 0$ the peak and rain coefficients differ sharply, and as $\lambda$ grows they converge. Ridge attributes a little to each rather than a lot to one.

Why this matters in production

Two adjacent road segments in a travel-time model are nearly the same feature. Attributing all the delay to one produces misleading segment-level diagnostics and a different answer after the next retrain. Splitting it is more stable.

5 · Lasso — the Laplace prior

Now use a Laplace (double-exponential) prior, $p(w_j) = \frac{1}{2b}\exp(-|w_j|/b)$:

$$ \log p(w) = -\frac{1}{b}\sum_{j=1}^p |w_j| + \text{const} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat w_{\text{lasso}} = \argmin_w \lVert y-Xw\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert w\rVert_1, \quad \lambda = \frac{2\sigma^2}{b}. $$

The objective is convex but not differentiable at $w_j = 0$, so there is no closed form. That non-differentiability is the mechanism that produces exact zeros.

5.1 · Deriving the soft-threshold by hand

Take one coordinate at a time. Hold $w_k$ fixed for $k \neq j$ and define the partial residual $r^{(j)} = y - \sum_{k\neq j} x_k w_k$ and $\rho_j = x_j^\top r^{(j)}$. The objective in $w_j$ alone is

$$ f(w_j) = \lVert r^{(j)} - x_j w_j\rVert_2^2 + \lambda|w_j| = \text{const} - 2\rho_j w_j + \lVert x_j\rVert^2 w_j^2 + \lambda|w_j|. $$
For $w_j > 0$: $f'= -2\rho_j + 2\lVert x_j\rVert^2 w_j + \lambda = 0 \Rightarrow w_j = (\rho_j - \lambda/2)/\lVert x_j\rVert^2$, valid only if $\rho_j > \lambda/2$.
For $w_j < 0$: symmetrically $w_j = (\rho_j + \lambda/2)/\lVert x_j\rVert^2$, valid only if $\rho_j < -\lambda/2$.
For $|\rho_j| \le \lambda/2$ neither branch is feasible, and $0$ belongs to the subdifferential. The minimiser is exactly $w_j = 0$.
Together: $\;\hat w_j = \dfrac{S(\rho_j,\;\lambda/2)}{\lVert x_j\rVert^2}$, where $S(\rho,\gamma) = \operatorname{sign}(\rho)\max(|\rho| - \gamma, 0)$.

Cycling this update over $j = 1,\dots,p$ until convergence is coordinate descent: about a dozen lines of code, and what scikit-learn runs.

5.2 · The geometry: why a diamond has corners

In constrained form, Lasso solves $\min \lVert y - Xw\rVert^2$ subject to $\lVert w\rVert_1 \le t$, and Ridge subject to $\lVert w\rVert_2^2 \le t$. The RSS contours are ellipses centred on $\hat w_{\text{OLS}}$. The solution is where the growing ellipse first touches the constraint set.

The $\ell_2$ ball is smooth, so first contact almost surely happens where all coordinates are non-zero. The $\ell_1$ ball is a diamond with vertices on the axes, and an expanding ellipse is far more likely to meet a corner than an edge. A corner means a coordinate is exactly zero: sparsity follows from the geometry.

5.3 · Three caveats worth stating out loud

  • Among correlated features the choice is arbitrary. If is_rain and traffic_speed carry the same information, Lasso keeps roughly one, and which one can change under resampling. The survivor is not evidence of a cause.
  • It saturates when $p > n$. Lasso selects at most $n$ variables, an awkward ceiling in genomics where $p$ is 20,000 and $n$ is 200.
  • The estimates are biased. Soft-thresholding shrinks the survivors too. If you need honest effect sizes, refit OLS on the selected set (the "relaxed" or debiased Lasso).

6 · Elastic Net — both priors at once

Multiply the two priors together — Gaussian times Laplace — and the log turns the product into a sum of penalties:

$$ \hat w_{\text{EN}} = \argmin_w \;\lVert y - Xw\rVert_2^2 \;+\; \lambda_1\lVert w\rVert_1 \;+\; \lambda_2\lVert w\rVert_2^2 . $$

The usual parameterisation writes $\lambda_1 = \alpha\rho$ and $\lambda_2 = \alpha(1-\rho)/2$: $\alpha$ sets the strength, and the mixing ratio $\rho \in [0,1]$ sets the character, with $\rho = 1$ pure Lasso and $\rho = 0$ pure Ridge.

The coordinate update inherits the soft-threshold, with the ridge term appearing in the denominator — one extra symbol relative to Lasso:

$$ \hat w_j \;\leftarrow\; \frac{S\!\left(\rho_j,\;\lambda_1\right)}{\lVert x_j\rVert^2 + \lambda_2}. $$

6.1 · The grouping effect

Zou & Hastie proved a bound worth carrying around. For standardized inputs and two features with sample correlation $r$, the Elastic Net solution satisfies

$$ \frac{|\hat w_i - \hat w_j|}{\lVert y\rVert_1} \;\le\; \frac{\sqrt{2(1-r)}}{\lambda_2}. $$

As $r \to 1$ the bound goes to zero, so perfectly correlated features receive identical coefficients. Lasso has no such guarantee. This is the formal version of "Lasso picks one, Elastic Net keeps the group".

Task 4 in one sentence

You will build is_bad_weather = is_rain AND traffic_speed < 25, which is correlated with both of its parents, then show that Lasso keeps roughly one of the three, Ridge keeps all three with similar weight, and Elastic Net keeps the group while still zeroing is_weekend. That last clause is the part Ridge cannot do.

6.2 · The double-shrinkage correction

Applying both penalties shrinks twice, once by $\ell_1$ and again by $\ell_2$, biasing the naïve solution toward zero. Zou & Hastie rescale by $(1+\lambda_2)$ to undo the second shrinkage; scikit-learn absorbs this into its parameterisation. If your coordinate descent disagrees with ElasticNet in the third decimal, the objective is the usual culprit.

7 · Past the point estimate

MAP returns the posterior mode and throws away the rest of the distribution. With a Gaussian prior and Gaussian likelihood the entire posterior is available in closed form:

$$ p(w \mid y, X) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma), \qquad \Sigma = \left(\frac{X^\top X}{\sigma^2} + \frac{I}{\tau^2}\right)^{-1}, \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\,\Sigma\, X^\top y . $$

The mean $\mu$ equals the Ridge solution at $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$, because a Gaussian's mode is its mean. Ridge is the posterior with the error bars discarded; the diagonal of $\Sigma$ returns them as $\hat w_j \pm 1.96\sqrt{\Sigma_{jj}}$.

For a new trip $x_\star$ the posterior predictive adds observation noise back:

$$ p(y_\star \mid x_\star, y) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x_\star^\top \mu,\; \underbrace{x_\star^\top \Sigma x_\star}_{\text{uncertainty in } w} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}\right). $$

The first term shrinks as you collect data; the second does not. That is the difference between uncertainty about the rain coefficient and the inherent noise in surges, and the reason a quote of ₹50 ± ₹10 is more honest than ₹50.

Three things to remember

Ridge shrinks, Lasso selects, Elastic Net does both. The penalty weight is a noise-to-prior variance ratio. And a coefficient whose credible interval covers zero is not a finding.
